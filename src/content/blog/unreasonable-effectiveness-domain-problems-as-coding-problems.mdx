In 1960, the physicist Eugene Wigner published an essay with one of the best titles in the history of ideas: "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences."[^1] His puzzle was simple and profound — why should mathematics, a product of human imagination, describe the physical universe so uncannily well? He called it "something bordering on the mysterious." In 2015, Andrej Karpathy borrowed the frame for "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks,"[^2] marvelling that a tiny character-level model could generate almost-readable Shakespeare. Both essays share a move: notice that a tool works far better than it has any right to, then ask why.

I want to make the same move for something I keep seeing in production. Most domain-specific problems — legal, commerce, finance — are unreasonably well suited to being reframed as coding problems. Not "solved by writing code," although sometimes that too. I mean reframed: expressed as structured extraction, executable invariants, deterministic verifiers, repair loops, and failure-driven evals.

This is the practical corollary of a thesis I keep repeating: [the harness is the moat, not the model](/blog/the-moat-is-the-harness-not-the-model). The model is rented. The harness is owned. And the best harness is made of code.

## Why Code Is the Perfect Substrate for Agentic Loops

Code has three properties no other medium for domain knowledge has — it's verifiable, composable, and executable — and those three are precisely what turn a stateless next-token predictor into a reliable agent. Get your domain onto the code substrate and you inherit all three for free.

- **Code is verifiable.** You can run it: a function returns the expected value or it doesn't, a schema validates or it throws, a test suite is green or red. No "the vibes are slightly off."
- **Code is composable.** Functions call functions, modules import modules — correctness composes. A prose paragraph doesn't; you can't `import` paragraph three into paragraph seven.
- **Code is executable.** It's not a description of an action, it *is* the action — a feedback signal at the end of every step.

<CodeSubstrateProperties />

- The 2026 survey "Code as Agent Harness" (Ning et al., 197-paper synthesis from UIUC / Meta / Stanford) makes this point at the research level: code is no longer just the *output* of an LLM, it's the *operational substrate* — "an executable and inspectable medium through which agents reason, act, and adapt."[^3]
- The survey's three layers: harness interface (code connects agent to reasoning, action, environment), harness mechanisms (planning, memory, tool use, feedback-driven control), and multi-agent scaling.[^3]
- The builder's framing: get your problem onto the code substrate and you inherit verifiability, composability, and executability for free.

## The Intellectual Lineage: Verifier's Law and RLVR

This reframing works *now* because of how the labs train: reinforcement learning against deterministic verifiers (RLVR) means the most verifiable tasks improve fastest. Verifier's law says every solvable, easily-verified task will be solved — and code hits all five of its trainability criteria.

- Jason Wei's July 2025 post "Asymmetry of verification and verifier's rule": some tasks are much easier to verify than to solve (Sudoku), and "with reinforcement learning (RL) that finally works in a general sense, asymmetry of verification is becoming one of the most important ideas in AI."[^4]

<Citation author="Jason Wei" source="Asymmetry of verification and verifier's rule, July 2025" type="quote">
Verifier's rule: The ease of training AI to solve a task is proportional to how verifiable the task is. All tasks that are possible to solve and easy to verify will be solved by AI.
</Citation>

- Wei's five trainability properties — objective truth, fast to verify, scalable to verify, low noise, continuous-ish reward — and code hits all five (a unit test is objective, fast, parallel, low-noise).[^4]
- RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards): train against a deterministic checker instead of a hackable learned reward model — "there is no reward hacking against a flawed reward model."[^5]
- Proof point 1: both OpenAI and Google DeepMind hit IMO gold-medal standard in July 2025 (35/42, five of six problems, human contest conditions) — via "general-purpose reinforcement learning and test-time compute scaling," per OpenAI's Alexander Wei.[^6]
- Proof point 2: SWE-bench Verified went from mid-tier scores to ~80% (Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro) by early 2026; OpenAI has essentially declared it saturated.[^7]
- CodeAct (Wang et al., ICML 2024): executable Python as the action space instead of rigid JSON tool calls → up to 20% higher success across 17 LLMs. Code composes; JSON doesn't.[^8]
- Anthropic now frames coding agents as the *universal* agent — "not a coding agent — an everything agent that happens to be best at code right now." Raschka's "Components of a Coding Agent": "the harness can often be the distinguishing factor that makes one LLM work better than another."[^9]
- The lineage: Wigner → Karpathy → verifier's law → RLVR → coding agents as universal agents. The synthesis: **your domain problem, expressed as a coding problem, plugs directly into this entire stack.**

## Worked Example 1 — Legal: Contract Review as Structured Extraction Plus a Deterministic Playbook

"Review this contract" is a vibes task. Reframed, it decomposes into two clean stages: structured extraction (prose → typed data, where the schema itself is a verifier) and a deterministic playbook (the firm's legal judgment as version-controlled, testable code). This is what Legora, Harvey, Kira, and Luminance converged on.

- The market has already converged here: Legora's "Tabular Review" extracts data points from thousands of contracts into structured tables with citations back to source;[^10] Harvey applies firm playbooks and grounds "every output in source paragraphs that the reviewer can verify";[^11] Kira and Luminance built the category on clause extraction.
- Intellectual roots: Stanford CodeX and Genesereth's computational law — legal rules "in computational form" so compliance can be checked mechanically.[^12]

The extraction step looks like this in Python with Pydantic:

```python
from pydantic import BaseModel
from datetime import date
from enum import Enum

class Governing(str, Enum):
    england_wales = "england_wales"
    sweden = "sweden"
    delaware = "delaware"

class Contract(BaseModel):
    counterparty: str
    effective_date: date
    term_months: int
    auto_renew: bool
    liability_cap_multiple: float | None   # cap as multiple of fees
    governing_law: Governing
    assignment_change_of_control: bool      # can counterparty assign on CoC?
```

The model populates the schema — and the schema itself is a verifier (wrong types throw, missing fields throw, out-of-enum jurisdictions throw). The playbook, where the actual legal judgment lives, is just code:

```python
def check_playbook(c: Contract) -> list[str]:
    issues = []
    if c.liability_cap_multiple is None or c.liability_cap_multiple > 2.0:
        issues.append("Liability cap exceeds 2x fees (playbook §4.1)")
    if c.auto_renew and c.term_months > 12:
        issues.append("Auto-renew on >12mo term needs approval (§7.2)")
    if c.assignment_change_of_control:
        issues.append("Assignment on change-of-control is non-standard (§9)")
    return issues
```

- The subjective task "is this contract acceptable?" became `extract → validate → run rules` — the playbook is version-controlled, testable, and owned by the firm.
- When a partner disagrees with a flag, they edit `§4.1` and the change is auditable — the difference between a demo and something a magic circle firm will deploy.
- LegalBench (162 legal reasoning tasks) exists precisely because the field learned to evaluate legal AI with structured tasks and reference answers, not vibes.[^13]
- The honest tension, per ContractKen: "this asymmetry — easy generation, hard verification — is the central bottleneck in production legal AI." The whole game is reducing verification cost; code is how.[^14]

## Worked Example 2 — Commerce: Decisions as Executable Invariants and Protocol Schemas

In commerce the reframing is happening industry-wide and in real time: business policy as unit-testable invariants that no prompt can jailbreak, and the entire buying journey being redefined as capability negotiation between typed schemas — ACP, UCP, AP2, MCP. The winner is whoever's catalog, cart, and checkout are cleanest as schemas.

"Should we upsell this customer, and with what?" As a coding problem, it's a set of executable invariants:

```typescript
type Cart = { items: LineItem[]; subtotal: number; currency: string };
type Offer = { sku: string; margin: number; inStock: boolean };

function chooseUpsell(cart: Cart, candidates: Offer[]): Offer | null {
  const eligible = candidates
    .filter(o => o.inStock)                    // never upsell out-of-stock
    .filter(o => o.margin > 0.15)              // margin floor
    .filter(o => cart.subtotal < 500);         // don't upsell big baskets
  return eligible.sort((a, b) => b.margin - a.margin)[0] ?? null;
}
```

- The LLM generates candidate offers and phrases them naturally; deterministic code decides *which* offer ships. The invariants can't be jailbroken by a clever prompt, and they can be unit-tested.
- September 2025: OpenAI + Stripe launch the Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP, Apache 2.0) — powers Instant Checkout in ChatGPT.[^15]
- January 2026: Google + Shopify launch the Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP), interoperable with AP2 (cryptographically signed payment mandates), A2A, and Anthropic's MCP. Merchants publish a JSON profile at `/.well-known/ucp`; agents compute the capability intersection.[^16]
- **Commerce is being redefined as capability negotiation between typed schemas**: Shopify's Catalog API "turns Shopify's global product catalog into structured, queryable infrastructure for AI agents"; Universal Cart "lets AI agents collect items from any merchant, on or off Shopify, into a single, unified cart."[^17]

<Citation author="Ilya Grigorik" source="Shopify Engineering, January 2026" type="pullquote">
Monolithic protocols eventually collapse under complexity: too rigid to adapt, too slow to evolve. Thoughtfully layered protocols survive and thrive by separating responsibilities, defining clear APIs, and enabling composition. TCP/IP proved this and UCP applies this pattern to commerce.
</Citation>

- That's a software architecture argument standing in for a commerce strategy — the whole thesis in one quote.
- Scale: over a million Shopify merchants (Glossier, SKIMS, Spanx, Vuori) brought into ChatGPT commerce, an audience OpenAI put at 700M+ weekly users; Salesforce: "48% of shoppers who already use AI for shopping are open to having an AI agent make a purchase for them."[^18]

## Worked Example 3 — Healthcare: Prior Authorization as Typed Resources Plus Payer Rules

Healthcare is running the same play with regulatory tailwind: FHIR gives the domain a type system, CMS-0057-F mandates machine-readable prior-authorization APIs, and payer criteria become procedure-level rules engines. The LLM extracts clinical evidence from messy notes; deterministic code decides whether the criteria are met.

- Prior authorization is the classic vibes task: a narrative letter arguing medical necessity, a human reviewer, weeks of waiting.
- The chart is already typed data: HL7 FHIR gives you `Patient`, `Condition`, `Observation`, and `Claim` resources — a schema surface for the entire clinical record.[^27]
- The LLM's job becomes structured extraction: diagnosis codes, failed prior therapies, lab values the payer's criteria require, with citations back to source notes; the payer's medical policy becomes deterministic procedure-level rules that match the evidence or return machine-readable gaps.
- Regulatory tailwind: CMS-0057-F requires Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, and CHIP payers to expose FHIR-based electronic prior-auth APIs — the vibes channel (fax a letter, wait) is being deprecated by regulation.[^26]
- Reported results: processing three to five times faster, denials from incomplete data roughly halved.[^26]
- Telling research detail: LLM-generated prior-auth letters score well on clinical content and poorly on administrative scaffolding — precisely the part that should be code, not prose.[^28]

## The Harness Pattern: Generate → Verify → Repair

Once your domain is on the code substrate, one loop works everywhere: the model proposes, a deterministic verifier disposes, and structured errors feed the repair attempt. Its power comes from three things that aren't the model — machine-readable errors, escalation as a feature, and domain experts contributing rules instead of prompts.

```python
def run(task, generate, verify, max_tries=3):
    errors = []
    for attempt in range(max_tries):
        candidate = generate(task, feedback=errors)
        errors = verify(candidate)          # returns [] if valid
        if not errors:
            return candidate, "ok"
    return candidate, "escalate"            # hand to a human
```

<GenerateVerifyRepairLoop />

- **Structured errors, not vibes.** `verify()` returns specific, machine-readable failures ("liability cap exceeds 2x", "SKU out of stock") that become the feedback for the next generation — the production-grade version of CodeAct's lab result that self-debugging agents outperform.[^8]
- **Escalation is a feature, not a failure.** After `max_tries`, hand off to a human with the full trail. In legal and finance, an honest "I need a human on §9" beats a confident wrong answer — the verifier defines the boundary of the system's competence.
- **Domain experts contribute rules, not prompts.** A lawyer edits `check_playbook`; a merchandiser edits the margin floor. Executable, testable, version-controlled artifacts instead of brittle prompts that silently regress.

## Failures Become Evals: The Eval Suite as Executable Specification

The compounding move: every production failure becomes a test case, and over time your eval suite becomes an executable specification of what "correct" means in your domain. When the next model drops, you don't guess whether to upgrade — you run the suite.

```yaml
# evals/contracts/regression.yaml
- id: cap-2024-11-liability
  input: fixtures/msa_acme.pdf
  expect_issues:
    - "Liability cap exceeds 2x fees (playbook §4.1)"
  must_not_flag:
    - "governing_law"          # was a false positive we fixed

- id: upsell-oos-2025-03
  input: fixtures/cart_holiday.json
  expect_upsell: null          # out-of-stock item must never be offered
```

```python
def test_regression(case, system):
    result = system.run(load(case["input"]))
    for issue in case.get("expect_issues", []):
        assert issue in result.issues
    for forbidden in case.get("must_not_flag", []):
        assert forbidden not in result.fields_flagged
```

- The same discipline that made software engineering tractable, ported to domain AI.
- The "Code as Agent Harness" survey lists "evaluation beyond final task success" and "regression-free harness improvement" as open challenges — research-speak for: your eval suite is the asset.[^3]
- When the next model drops, run the suite. Green means ship. That's the difference between operating a serious system and vibing.

## This Isn't New: Rules as Code Got There First

The reframing predates LLMs by decades. Catala compiles French tax law into executable code at the DGFiP, the OECD runs a rules-as-code program for machine-consumable legislation, and domain-driven design has preached "ubiquitous language" since 2003. What's new is that LLMs finally solve the expensive half — translating messy reality into the formalism.

- **Catala**: an Inria-designed DSL for writing tax and benefits law as literate programs, built on *prioritized default logic* — a semantics matching the base-case-plus-exceptions structure of statutes, readable by lawyers and programmers side by side.[^23]
- The French Public Finance Directorate (DGFiP) uses it to make income-tax computation auditable; Lawsky's tax scholarship shows the encoding process surfaces ambiguities the prose silently hides.[^24]
- The OECD generalized the idea as "Rules as Code": publish a machine-consumable version of government rules alongside the natural-language one, co-drafted as twins.[^25]
- Software engineering's version: Evans' *Domain-Driven Design* (2003) — the domain model lives in the code as a "ubiquitous language" shared by experts and engineers — which is exactly what a Pydantic `Contract` or a FHIR resource is.[^31] Stanford CodeX has made the legal version of the argument since the 2000s.[^12]
- Why rules-as-code stayed niche for twenty years: the bottleneck was never executing the rules — it was *translating messy reality into the formalism*. Hand-keying every invoice, contract, and clinical note into the schema was the expensive, error-prone half.
- **That's the half LLMs just solved.** Structured extraction at near-zero marginal cost is the missing adapter between unstructured inputs and the executable rules the domain always wanted to run. Rules as code was waiting for a universal parser; now it has one.

<Callout type="insight" title="The division of labor that works">
LLMs handle the boundary between the messy world and the schema. Code handles the judgment once the data is typed. Twenty years of rules-as-code failed by asking humans to do the first half; two years of vibes-based LLM products failed by asking the model to do the second. The unreasonable effectiveness lives in doing both halves on the substrate each is best at.
</Callout>

## Why the Moat Argument Holds

Models commoditize on a 94.5% price-collapse curve; verifiers, loops, and evals compound. Better: because the labs train against verifiable rewards, every model upgrade disproportionately improves exactly the capabilities your code-substrate harness depends on. Express your domain as code and you ride the steepest part of the capability curve for free.

- **Models commoditize, fast.** BenchLM.ai (July 2026): average frontier output price down ~94.5% since March 2023, price index from 100 to 5.5.[^19] Stanford AI Index 2025: GPT-3.5-equivalent query cost fell from $20.00 to $0.07 per million tokens in ~18 months — a 280-fold reduction.[^20] Gartner (March 2026): trillion-parameter inference over 90% cheaper by 2030.[^21]
- Whatever frontier model you pay for today, an equal or better one will be dramatically cheaper soon — and you'll swap it with an environment-variable change.
- **Verifiers, loops, and evals compound.** Schemas, playbook code, repair loops, regression suites — accumulated, version-controlled, domain-specific judgment that appreciates as failures accrete into tests. Expensive for a competitor to reproduce.
- **The second-order effect that seals it:** the labs train on verifiable tasks (the RLVR mechanism), so every model upgrade disproportionately improves exactly what your harness depends on — reading structure, emitting valid schemas, using execution feedback, writing code.
- Builders on the vibes substrate — soft prose in, soft prose out — get the least benefit from each upgrade, because that's the jagged, least-verifiable edge of the frontier, the part the training loops improve last.[^22]
- Karpathy closed the loop on his own 2015 essay: if a task is verifiable, it is optimizable — directly or via RL — and that's what drives the jagged shape of LLM progress. The jaggedness maps to verification cost; this reframing moves your product to the cheap side of the map.[^22]

## Where the Reframing Breaks: The Honest Limits

Three failure modes to respect: verifiers can be gamed (Goodhart's law survives contact with RLVR — held-out evals and continual audit are non-negotiable), some judgment resists formalization (taste, strategy, negotiation), and premature formalization can freeze the wrong ontology into your schemas. The mitigation for all three is the same: keep humans on the escalation path and treat the verifier itself as versioned, evolving code.

- **Verifiers can be gamed.** SpecBench (2026) separates visible from held-out tests and shows high validation scores substantially overestimate true specification compliance — agents overfit visible tests, retrieve leaked answers, or modify the verifier itself.[^29] "The Verification Horizon" pushes it to the theoretical limit: Rice's theorem plus Goodhart's law guarantee proxy-based verification fails eventually, and the failure rate *rises* with agent capability.[^30]
  - The consequence is discipline, not despair: hold out tests the generation loop never sees, audit the verifier as adversarially as the output, treat "the suite is green" as strong evidence rather than proof.
- **Some judgment resists the schema.** The jagged frontier cuts both ways:[^31] whether a joke lands, whether a repositioning is wise, whether opposing counsel will blink — no `assert` exists, and pretending otherwise produces confident nonsense wearing a type signature.
  - The narrower, stronger version of the thesis: inside almost every "unverifiable" domain job there's a large verifiable core (extraction, consistency, policy compliance, arithmetic, citation grounding) and a small judgment-shaped residue. Express the core as code; route the residue to humans.
- **Premature formalization freezes the wrong ontology.** A schema is a bet about what matters; encode your playbook before you understand your domain and you automate your misunderstandings at scale. Even statutes — the most formalization-ready text we have — hide genuine ambiguities that encoding surfaces rather than resolves.[^24]
  - Law in particular is *contested*, not merely computed: sometimes the right answer to "what does §4.1 mean?" is litigation, not a function. Treat schemas and rules like code — versioned, reviewed, revisited — and let the eval loop tell you where your ontology is wrong.

<Callout type="warning" title="The rule of thumb">
If a domain expert can tell you, concretely, why an output is wrong — that reason can usually become code. If the best they can offer is "it doesn't feel right," you've found the residue that stays human. Build the boundary there, and re-survey it every quarter, because the verifiable core grows.
</Callout>

## How to Start on Monday

You don't need a platform, a protocol, or permission — the reframing starts with one workflow, one schema, and one rule file. Five steps, each shippable in a week.

1. **Pick one decision your domain makes repeatedly** — a contract clause check, an eligibility call, an offer selection. Not the whole job; one decision with a knowable right answer.
2. **Write the schema first.** Define the typed data that decision actually consumes — it forces the domain conversation ("what *is* a liability cap, exactly?") that prompts let you skip.
3. **Port the policy out of heads and into a rules file.** Interview whoever currently makes the call and turn their reasons into `check_*` functions. Expect arguments; the arguments are the point — your domain's ambiguities surfacing at design time instead of in production.
4. **Wire the loop.** LLM extracts into the schema, rules run, structured errors feed the repair attempt, `max_tries` escalates to a human with the trail. Twenty lines of orchestration.
5. **Capture every failure as an eval.** From day one, before you have scale. The suite is the asset that makes model upgrades a green-diff decision instead of a leap of faith.

## Conclusion

Wigner's mystery was why mathematics fits nature. Ours is smaller and has an answer: domain problems fit code because the industry spent two years making code the best-trained, best-verified, cheapest-to-check substrate an LLM can operate on. The builders who reframe first inherit the whole stack.

- Wigner never resolved his puzzle; ours dissolves under inspection, and that's what makes it actionable. Code is effective for domain problems because verification is the one signal that scales, the labs aim their training loops at whatever can be verified, and sixty years of software tooling — types, tests, version control, CI — is exactly the harness a probabilistic model needs.
- The move available to every builder in every vertical: find the verifiable core of your domain, give it a type system, write the judgment down as rules, wrap the loop, and let the failures accrete into evals.
- The model is rented. The schemas, the playbook, the verifiers, the regression suite — those are owned, and they appreciate.

<Callout type="insight">
Your domain didn't get easier. You moved it onto the substrate where every model upgrade, every RLVR run, and every dollar of lab capex works for you — for free. That's not mysterious. That's leverage.
</Callout>
